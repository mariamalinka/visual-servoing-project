# Learned worker lifetime diagnostics

Completed 25–26 September 2026 on the same computer. The statistical comparison used 32 fresh workers and eight reused workers, interleaved in eight blocks. Reused sessions ran for 120 seconds each. Every fresh worker completed one alignment. Controller, offset, model, calibration, scene, dependencies and initialization/warm-up matched the preserved scheduling campaign fingerprint. Added transport remained 50 ms, freshness 400 ms and control deadline 50 ms. No application changes or production restart strategy were introduced.

These are uncached per-frame processing percentiles grouped by alignment position, including failed and late frames; they are not total alignment duration. The 356 later attempts are clustered within eight workers, not 356 independent workers. Cached-frame timings do not mask inference latency. Raw frame checksums and full distributions are in comparison.json. All recorded motion-safety checks passed; stops stayed latched and there were no unsafe or post-stop motion ticks.

Continuous GPU telemetry contains 6,190 samples. Among 430 samples strictly inside processing intervals of at least 200 ms, median SM clock was 95 MHz (p95 247 MHz; max 652 MHz), versus 247 MHz median for frames below 120 ms. Slow-frame states included P0, P3 and P5; P0 alone did not imply high clocks. Slow-frame temperature peaked at 55 C; VRAM was constant at 361 MiB. This is a correlation, not evidence proving thermal throttling, a power brake, or a driver bug. Sampling at 250 ms does not resolve every short clock transition.

The separate native diagnostic reproduced degradation (11/36 alignments, 25 freshness trips, zero control misses). It is excluded from the table. WPR recorded CPU switches, ready/wait stacks, sampled CPU stacks, GPU scheduling and power events. The 80-second fallback initiated recording; slow-frame detection subsequently fired during capture. No lost events or buffers were reported. Actual decoded target-thread coverage was about 21.9 seconds. A 321.5 ms frame overlapping recorder startup is retained and flagged, not used to attribute ordinary degraded-state behavior.

Across seven slow frames entirely between recorder startup and shutdown, the sensor thread spent 1,520.3 ms running, 142.2 ms ready but undispatched, and 10.4 ms blocked. CPU interval accounting closes to each frame's wall time. Thus about 93% of their off-CPU time was scheduler dispatch delay, not blocked device/lock waits. In a representative 270.1 ms frame, 236.7 ms was running, 33.3 ms ready and 0 ms blocked; approximately 199.9 ms of sampled CPU weight landed in nvcuda64.dll. Across the seven frames, nvcuda64.dll accounted for roughly 79% of sampled CPU weight, with call paths through PyTorch, CUDA runtime and the NVIDIA user-mode driver. Extract/match CUDA stream spans in the representative frame were 121.4/135.8 ms.

This narrows the bottleneck to the CUDA/driver execution path plus secondary CPU dispatch delay; Python computation and blocked locks do not dominate these recorded slow frames. However, module-level stacks do not distinguish driver busy polling from driver computation, and CUDA stream spans do not separate GPU execution, queueing or preemption. Fresh-worker success does not distinguish an application/context-lifecycle interaction from driver power/scheduling behavior. The underlying cause of the low-clock regime remains unproven. No control deadline miss reproduced in either test, so this trace cannot identify the source of the previously observed control overruns.
